Why HBM bandwidth stops scaling: die edge, then power

"The interposer solves wires per stack, so beachfront then caps the number of stacks. After that, energy per bit binds."

The sentence is right in outline, with two corrections. "Wires per stack" and "stacks per die" are one limit: wires per millimetre of die edge. And power is a limit at the same time, not only "after".

A silicon interposer lets one memory attachment carry 2,048 data wires instead of a DDR channel's 64. Those wires are wide, single-ended and unterminated, so they only reach a few millimetres. The host's HBM PHY therefore has to sit at a die edge, facing the stack. Bandwidth then depends on two things: how many wires fit per millimetre of edge, and how much edge a reticle-limited die has, which is room for about six stacks. Getting more edge means more dies, and interposer size runs out. Even with more stacks, today's energy per bit would push memory power above the GPU's own power budget long before bandwidth gets anywhere near on-chip SRAM levels.

1. The interposer buys width per attachment

Off-package, every bit crosses solder balls about 1 mm apart, centimetres of board and a DIMM connector. A DDR5 channel is two 32-bit subchannels: 64 data bits, plus ECC on RDIMMs. It costs a CPU roughly 160–200 signal pins. On a silicon interposer, connections are microbumps tens of microns apart and routes are millimetres long, so one HBM4 stack gets 2,048 data wires.

Per attachment, that is about 32× the wires and about 38× the bandwidth. The rate per wire barely differs: MI455X's HBM4 works out to ≈7.6 Gb/s per pin, against DDR5-6400's 6.4 [D]. Nearly all the gain is width.

2. Why the stack must sit at a die edge, and what that caps

Reach, not energy, pins the stack to the edge. An HBM link is a very wide parallel bus of single-ended, unterminated lines [V: SK hynix HC'26, "1024 IOs (Single-ended)"]. On fine-pitch interposer wiring it only works over a few millimetres. Samsung's floorplans give channel depths of 3.5 mm (HBM2), 4.5 mm (HBM3) and 5.5 mm (HBM4) [V: Samsung HC'26 s11].

A stack placed farther away does not just cost more energy: the standard HBM PHY stops working, and a serialized, longer-reach interface is needed instead. JEDEC's SPHBM4 makes exactly that trade, with 512 signals at 4:1 serialization on an organic substrate [S].

Width per stack and stacks per die are therefore the same limit. Doubling HBM to 2,048 DQ meant widening the PHY from 6 mm to 8 mm and lengthening the channel from 3.5 to 5.5 mm, going from HBM2 to standard HBM4 [V: Samsung s11]. About 2,048 DQ across an ~8 mm PHY is roughly 256 DQ per mm of die edge [D]. Samsung names "B-die PHY I/O count and speed" and TSV counts as the main per-stack bottlenecks [V: s5].

How many stacks fit.

  • A monolithic die cannot exceed the ~26 × 33 mm reticle.
  • An HBM4 stack's footprint is about 11 × 12.8 mm [V: Samsung s10–11]. A die's two 33 mm edges therefore hold about three stacks each, six in total. Samsung draws it that way [V: s9].
  • The die edge is also shared with die-to-die links and off-package I/O.
  • More stacks need more dies, and so a bigger interposer or bridge package. Samsung says the "interposer size [is] reaching its physical limit" [V: s9]. d-Matrix cites "interposer size, warpage" [V: s5].

3. Power binds alongside: P = BW × energy per bit

Each HBM generation has cut energy per bit, but more slowly than bandwidth has risen, so memory power keeps climbing. Figure 3 replots d-Matrix's chart of 8-stack package bandwidth against energy per bit, with lines of constant power added. HBM generations climb from about 50 W to about 350 W [D]. Reaching 100 TB/s would take:

  • At HBM4's 2.4 pJ/bit (no further improvement): 1.92 kW for memory alone [V: d-Matrix s6].
  • If the historical HBM trend continues (~1.4 pJ/bit): about 1.15 kW [D: log-log fit of the five HBM points].
  • At 5 pJ/bit, the figure d-Matrix uses for HBM4 in its own like-for-like table (40 mW per GB/s, including on-chip delivery): about 4 kW [D from V: s28].

For scale, Samsung's example GPU draws 1.2 kW of compute power, plus about 220 W of DRAM at ~14 TB/s [V: Samsung s23]. O'Connor made the same point in 2017: a 60 W DRAM budget caps HBM2 at about 2 TB/s [P]. Power has been a co-limit all along, not just the next wall after beachfront.

4. The escape route, and its price

3D stacking replaces edge with area: the interface runs through the face of the die rather than its perimeter. Samsung's zHBM projection reaches about 32 TB/s from only four stacks once they are bonded onto the xPU [V]. d-Matrix's Raptor reaches 100 TB/s at 0.37 pJ/bit of measured I/O energy [V]. The costs move elsewhere:

  • Capacity per area. Raptor holds 11.4 MB/mm², against 21.9–26.3 for HBM4 [V: d-Matrix s28].
  • Heat. In Raptor the logic sits on top and the DRAM underneath, inside the logic's thermal footprint. At 105 °C retention falls from 32 ms to 4 ms, so refresh runs 8× as often [V: s17, s25–26]. In DRAM-over-logic designs such as zHBM, the heat also has to cross the DRAM.

Interface energy needs a like-for-like comparison.

  • Raptor's 0.37 pJ/bit is measured I/O.
  • The HBM2 interposer wire alone is ~0.3 pJ/bit [P: O'Connor].
  • An HBM interface including its PHY is ~1.5 pJ/bit [P: UniIC IEDM'20].

Measured against the full HBM interface, the I/O gain is about 4×, not d-Matrix's "~10×". Most of an HBM access is spent inside the DRAM anyway: 1.21 pJ/bit on activation and 2.24 pJ/bit on moving data within the die [P: O'Connor].

The general principle. Bandwidth has to cross a boundary: in 2.5D that boundary is an edge, a length; in 3D it is a face, an area. Compute grows with area. 2.5D memory bandwidth grows with edge length × wires per mm × rate per wire. Each bit costs energy roughly in proportion to how far it travels and how much circuitry it passes through. Each step closer changes which of these limits binds.

What to remember

  • The interposer raises width per attachment about 32× (2,048 vs 64 data wires). The rate per wire barely changes.
  • The link reaches only a few millimetres, so bandwidth is capped by wires per mm of die edge: about six stacks per reticle-sized die. More dies run into interposer size limits.
  • Power is a co-limit. 100 TB/s costs ~1.1–1.9 kW of memory power at HBM-class energy (≈4 kW at d-Matrix's like-for-like figure), against a GPU that draws ~1.2 kW itself.
  • 3D stacking swaps edge for area and escapes both limits, at a cost in capacity per mm² and heat.
