Why HBM bandwidth stops scaling: wires, then beachfront, then energy

"The interposer solves wires per stack, so beachfront then caps the number of stacks. After that, energy per bit binds."

Package memory bandwidth is the product of three numbers: how many wires reach each memory attachment, how fast each wire runs, and how many attachments fit beside the compute die. The power it costs is that bandwidth times the energy of each bit. Moving DRAM from a DIMM onto a silicon interposer multiplies the first number by about 32. That pushes the limit onto the third number. Even if more stacks could be fitted, the power would become the limit next.

Read left to right: each step removes the limit before it and exposes the next. In today's packages, limits 2 and 3 apply at the same time (§4).

1. The interposer solves wires per attachment

Off-package, every bit has to cross the package through solder balls about 1 mm apart, travel centimetres of board, and pass through a DIMM connector. A DDR5 channel carries 64 data bits (two 32-bit subchannels, plus ECC on RDIMMs) and costs a CPU roughly 160–200 signal pins. On a silicon interposer, connections are microbumps a few tens of microns apart and the wires are millimetres long. One HBM4 stack gets 2,048 data wires.

2. Beachfront caps the number of stacks

Each stack's PHY has to sit against a compute-die edge and connect over a short interposer route. Every millimetre of interposer trace costs about 0.5 pJ/bit [V: d-Matrix s8], so a stack moved 10 mm farther out would add roughly 5 pJ/bit [D], about double HBM4's whole budget. Stacks therefore line the die edges, and the number of stacks is set by usable edge length. Making the interposer larger to expose more edge runs into "interposer size, warpage" [V: d-Matrix s5]. Today's packages carry 8–12 stacks, which d-Matrix puts at a practical limit of about 20 TB/s with HBM4.

3. Energy per bit binds next

Each HBM generation has bought more bandwidth by moving to more aggressive I/O and lower energy per bit. Energy per bit has fallen more slowly than bandwidth has risen, though, so total memory power keeps climbing. Figure 3 replots d-Matrix's chart of 8-stack package bandwidth against data-movement energy and overlays lines of constant power, P = BW × 8 bit/B × Ebit. HBM generations climb from about 50 W to about 350 W of memory power [D]. Getting to 100 TB/s along the HBM trend at 2.4 pJ/bit would take 1.92 kW for HBM alone [V], more than Samsung's entire 1.2 kW package budget in its own example [V: Samsung HC'26 s23].

4. What the sentence gets right, and where it is loose

Right. The interposer does raise wires per attachment by about 32×. Stack count is limited by die edge and interposer size, which d-Matrix states directly. And power is what stops the obvious fix of adding stacks: 5× today's bandwidth at HBM4's energy would cost more than a whole package's power.

Loose.

  • "After that" is a logical order, not a time order. In current packages beachfront and power bind together. d-Matrix: "SOTA packages neither have beachfront, nor power for SRAM-like bandwidth" [V].
  • Bandwidth per stack is not fully solved. d-Matrix also notes that "pin speed, IO width (per HBM base die) have improved slowly" [V]. That is why HBM4 doubled the interface to 2,048 bits instead of only raising the rate.
  • Memory power is not all wire energy. Most of an HBM2 access is spent inside the DRAM: 2.24 of 3.92 pJ/bit is on-die data movement and 1.21 is activation, against 0.3 for the interposer wire [P: O'Connor MICRO'17]. Edge-attached HBM also forces data to cross the compute die over the on-chip network; d-Matrix lists "+3 pJ (on chip)" for 2.5D HBM4 [V].
The general principle. Bandwidth crosses a boundary: an edge for 2.5D, a face for 3D. Compute grows with area. Wires grow with the length of the boundary divided by the connection pitch, and each bit costs energy roughly in proportion to how far it travels. Each step toward the compute changes which of these limits binds. Going vertical (3D DRAM-on-logic) replaces edge with area and drops interface energy to ~0.3–0.4 pJ/bit. It then pays in capacity per mm² (about half of HBM4's [V]) and in heat that has to pass through the DRAM (retention falls from 32 ms to 4 ms at 105 °C [V]).
