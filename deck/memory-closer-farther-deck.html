01 · Question and answer

Closer and farther are not opposites: both leave DDR's narrow spot on one cost frontier.

Why are tighter memory–compute coupling (A) and memory disaggregation / far memory (B) both attractive?
Answer
1

Distance is not what is optimized.

2

A answers the bandwidth pressure: more GB/s per GB, fewer pJ/bit (HBM, 3D DRAM–logic stacking, PIM).

B answers capacity cost: cheaper, extended or shared GB (compression, CXL, RDMA pools).

3

Skew is within each workload, so mixed tiers win; the DDR middle persists.

4

The directions are coupled, and since ~2010 slower $/bit declines no longer let the middle outrun both.

Latency is a separate constraint (slide 2); skew decides the mix (slide 6).

02 · Distance is a proxy

Distance is a proxy: closer is not lower‑latency, and farther costs hops, not metres.

A 6 ft (~2 m) cable adds ~12 ns each way. Latency lives in device timing, controllers, SerDes, switches and queues.

Measured idle latency (filled) vs spec or modelled estimates (hollow). Platforms differ; compare within a row. NVMe is a typical value. Grace and GPU rows are [S] approximations, confounded by each host's cache hierarchy.

Closer ≠ faster. On Xeon Max, the same cores see ~20% higher pointer-chase latency from HBM2e than from DDR5, yet stream ~700 vs ~200 GB/s.
Farther = hops. One CXL hop adds ~100–280 ns (measured); a switch adds ≥220 ns moreV.
"Far" is a role. GB200's capacity tier (LPDDR5X) sits beside the Grace CPU, not out on a far network.

03 · Pressure 1: bandwidth → A

Bandwidth must cross a boundary; each step closer adds wires, cuts pJ/bit, until another limit binds.

Bandwidth = wires crossing a boundary × rate per wire. Memory power = bandwidth × energy per bit.

Compute outgrows DRAM bandwidth ~1.9× per 2 years [D: 3.0/1.6].

Fitted rates as stated by Gholami et al.; lines, not data points.

At most ~6 HBM4 stacks fit a reticle die's two long edges.

To scale, after Samsung HC'26 s9–s11. More dies → bigger interposer (size, warpage limits).

Replotted from d-Matrix HC'26 s6 (±5% read). HBM = 8-stack package; DDR/LPDDR ≈ one 64-bit channel; GDDR6 = 384-bit bus.
d-Matrix labels HBM4's 2–3 pJ/bit "I/O", "data-movement" and "system" on different slides; its HBM2 point (≈5.6 pJ/bit) uses its own, different scope. Raptor 0.37 = measured I/O only; zHBM = Samsung 4-stack projection (HBM points 8-stack): 0.49 = total DRAM power ÷ peak BW, utilisation unstated, so a lower bound; Samsung's HBM4E baseline ≈2.0.

After the interposer, most energy sits in the DRAM core (O'Connor's HBM2 model: 3.45 of 3.92 pJ/bit, full access, modelled; newer generations lower it). 3D stacking removes the edge limit, but HBM2 I/O is already 0.3–0.8 pJ/bit by toggle rate (interposer-wire term only), vs Raptor's 0.37: core energy binds next (→ PIM, slide 7).

04 · Pressure 2: capacity cost → B

Capacity cost now bites: $/bit declines slowed ~2.5–3× and memory reached 37% of Meta's rack TCO.

Bits stopped getting cheap fast while core counts kept growing, and much of the memory bought sits idle.

Memory share of rack TCO and power across six Meta generations (data labels as printed). TPP, ASPLOS'23, Fig. 3.

$/bit decline:~33–37%/yr before ~2010; ~12–15%/yr since (chart: slide 9).
Capacity per unit of CPU performance:fell ~60–70% in 10 years.
Idle bytes:32% of Google fleet memory is cold at 120 s; 55–80% of Meta app memory is idle per 2 min.

06 · Skew and placement

Access is skewed within each workload, so a mix of tiers beats any single tier.

A small hot head of the bytes carries most of the traffic; the tail stays cold for minutes.

Each point restates one published statistic as (share of bytes, share of activity). Metrics and windows differ; do not compare points with each other. [D]

ObjectGoes toExamples
High ρ, low IA: HBM (PIM proposed)Decode attention over live KV (~1 FLOP/B × GQA group); bulk scans
High ρ, high IOn-chip SRAMPrefill (~1000 FLOP/B); dense GEMM
Low ρ, high MLPB: CXL, compressed, pooledCold pages; most embedding rows; reusable KV
Low ρ, low MLPDDR busPointer chasing, B-trees, graphs (GAPBS worst on CXL)
ρ (access density) = GB/s demanded per GB stored: the demand-side twin of slide 5's axis. I = FLOP per byte moved · MLP = memory-level parallelism.

Low arithmetic intensity alone does not decide placement: embedding gathers are 0.25 FLOP/B, yet the big tables have low bandwidth demand.

Mixing pays: production moves ~20–25% of DRAM to cheaper tiers (zswap, TMTS's Optane, Vistara's CXL) at <5% slowdown; TPP: 33% in emulation; local DRAM keeps 50–75%.

08 · Coupling

The directions are coupled: A needs capacity tiers and competes for wafers; B pulls compute outward.

A → capacity tiers
≈43 vs ≈0.82 GB/s per GB [D]
GB200 NVL72 rack, NVIDIA spec [V] (colour = role: capacity tier)

Closer trades capacity for bandwidth, so a capacity tier follows.

A ↔ B share wafers
TrendForce, 2 June 2026 press release [S]: end-of-year wafer share; 2026 est. (June 2026), 2027 forecast.

HBM3E takes ~3× the wafer per bit (HBM4E expected >4:1), a named 2026-shortage driver. New-DRAM B tiers pay more; wafer-free B (recycled DDR4, compression) gains. The shortage did not cause B.

B → compute at the far (capacity) tier
  • Deployed only in training (optimizer at the capacity tier: ZeRO, MemoryX).
  • Elsewhere: simulations (CXL-PNM, BEACON) or vendor claims (XCENAV).

Catch: a CXL controller sees channel-level, not bank-level, bandwidth.

Rival explanations, weighed

Different markets: first-order, yet single workloads (LLM serving, GB200) use both, so markets differ because their binding constraint differs.

Vendor incentives: real (escaping commodity pricing), but HMC, Gen-Z and The Machine show incentives alone don't make a direction work.
