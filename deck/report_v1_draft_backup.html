Closer and farther at once

Why tighter memory–compute coupling and memory disaggregation are both attractive. Report built from SYNTHESIS_v1.4, the deck's reconciled figure data and the round-1 review decisions.

Abstract. Memory is moving toward compute (HBM, 3D DRAM–logic stacking, processing in memory) and away from it (CXL expansion and pooling, compressed tiers, RDMA far memory) at the same time. We argue that distance is not the quantity being optimized. Both directions leave the DDR DIMM's narrow region of a cost frontier whose axes are bandwidth supplied per GB stored and $/GB, under a latency constraint. Bandwidth must cross a boundary, and the boundary's wires and energy per bit push the hot head of the data toward compute. A slowed $/bit decline pushes the cold tail onto cheaper bytes. Because access is skewed within each workload, a mix of tiers beats any single tier, and the DDR middle persists. The deployed forms need no new programming model; compute at memory and cross-host pooling remain niche. The two directions are coupled through capacity demand and shared DRAM wafers.

01 · Question and answer

The question and the answer

Closer and farther are not opposites: both leave DDR's narrow spot on one cost frontier.

The question. Two directions in memory systems look opposed. Direction A, tighter memory–compute coupling, includes high-bandwidth memory (HBM) on a silicon interposer, 3D DRAM–logic stacking with hybrid or face-to-face bonding, and processing in memory or near-data processing (PIM/NDP). Direction B, disaggregation or far memory, includes CXL Type-3 memory expanders on one host, CXL memory pools shared by several hosts, compressed memory tiers such as zswap, and remote memory reached over RDMA. Both attract research and product investment at the same time. Why?

The answer in four parts [Syn]:

  1. Distance is not the variable being optimized. Each memory tier is a bundle of three properties: bandwidth supplied per GB stored, cost per GB, and latency. A and B both move away from the region the DDR DIMM occupies.
  2. Two pressures act on that bundle. A answers the bandwidth pressure: more GB/s per GB and fewer pJ per bit. B answers the capacity-cost pressure: cheaper or shared GB.
  3. Access is skewed within each workload. A small hot head of the bytes carries most of the traffic, so a mix of tiers beats any single tier, and the DDR middle persists between the two ends.
  4. The directions are coupled, through capacity demand and shared DRAM wafers, and the timing has a cause: since about 2011, cheap DRAM no longer lets the middle outrun both ends, as it did in the 1990s.

How the thesis evolved. The first draft (v0.1) explained both directions with a single "access density" axis. The first adversarial review (notes/r2d_review.md, F1–F3) found three faults. It conflated access density (bytes per second demanded per byte stored, ρ) with arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved, I). It ignored that every tier bundles bandwidth with capacity and price, so no single axis orders them. And it treated deployed B as "far", when Meta's and Google's deployed B tiers sit in the same server. The fix was the two-axis frontier with latency as a constraint, and the split of each direction into a deployed and an ambitious form (§7). A second review (notes/r5_review_v04.md) corrected the PIM baselines, scoped the pooling claims, and replaced an HBM "price floor" with "reallocation pressure" (§8). A three-round review of the HBM explainer then rewrote the bandwidth argument (§3).

Confidence [Syn]. High for the deployed forms: HBM without in-stack compute (A1) and single-host capacity tiers (B1) are in volume production. Medium for the coupling claims (§8), which rest on vendor statements and analyst shares. Low for the economics of compute at memory (A2) and cross-host pooling (B2), which are mostly simulated or modelled.

What the slide simplified. The slide names the three B examples and the three A examples without saying which are deployed; §7 does that. Its schematic has no numbers by design, and its A arrow points up and to the right, which assumes A always costs more per GB. That holds for HBM [P/V] and is unknown for 3D DRAM–logic stacks, whose $/GB nobody has published.

02 · Distance is a proxy

Distance is a proxy

Distance is a proxy: closer is not faster, and farther costs hops, not metres.

The physics of a memory access

Signals travel at roughly 5 ns per metre in copper [background]; Levis et al. note that a 6 ft cable adds 12 ns each way [P]1, which is 6.6 ns/m [D]. A load that leaves a CPU core spends most of its time elsewhere: in the core's cache hierarchy and on-chip network (75–170 ns of the measured CPU side in Octopus's analyser breakdown [P]2), in the DRAM device itself (about 50 ns worst case unloaded [P]15; Octopus measured 35–40 ns of DRAM access [P]2), and in every serializer, controller and switch on the path. Das Sharma et al. give 21 ns (common reference clock) to 25 ns (independent clock) per CXL port round trip, paid twice per access, for a 57 ns link adder against an 80 ns specification target [P/V, spec]9. A retimer adds about 10 ns per round trip in Das Sharma's table and about 10 ns per direction in Berger's model [P, estimate]9,10. Berger's model assumes each switch adds at least 70 ns for ports, arbitration and network-on-chip [P, modelled]10. None of these terms scales with metres.

DRAM never bought latency with its scaling either. Over the eleven years Chang studied, tRCD fell 30.5% and tRC 26.3% while $/bit fell 16× [P]17. Moving memory closer changes the number of wires and the energy per bit (§3); it changes the device's timing very little.

Closer is not faster

The cleanest test holds the cores and the on-chip network fixed and changes only the memory. Intel's Xeon Max 9468 does this: each of its four tiles has 12 cores, one HBM2e stack (16 GB, 409.6 GB/s peak) and two DDR5 channels (32 GB, 76.8 GB/s peak). Vaverka et al. measure about 20% higher single-core pointer-chase latency from HBM than from DDR, and the penalty stays constant up to 48 cores; achieved bandwidth is about 700 GB/s from HBM against about 200 GB/s from DDR5 [P, measured]13. HBM wins only once enough accesses are in flight. The paper prints no absolute nanoseconds; a linear read of its Fig. 3 gives roughly 98 ns (DDR) and 122 ns (HBM) at the largest window, a single read we did not plot [D, ±4 ns].

GPU and Grace numbers cannot be compared with CPU-to-DDR numbers like for like. H100 sees about 330 ns to HBM3 and GH200's GPU "under 300 ns", while Grace sees "over 200 ns" to its LPDDR5X [S]14. Those figures include different cache hierarchies and pointer-chase methods.

Loaded latency and tails

Idle numbers flatter every tier. COAXIAL measures DDR5-4800 at 60% utilization at 160 ns average and 285 ns p90 [P]15. Melody finds p99.99 and p99.999 latencies of CXL devices above 700 ns, reaching about 1 µs on two devices [P]3. Vistara's CXL tier is +92% idle (250 vs 130 ns) and +59% loaded (372 vs 234 ns at 60% utilization) relative to local DDR5 [P]4. Sun et al. report tail-latency increases of 10–82% for microsecond-scale applications placed on CXL [P]20.

Counter-evidence

Two results complicate the ranking. Colloid shows that under load the default tier's latency "can inflate to be 2.5× larger" than the alternate tier's, and that cores can generate 3–9× more traffic than the memory interconnect carries; in that regime a CXL tier is a bandwidth source, and hottest-pages-first placement can be 2.3× worse than optimal [P]18. Berger's TC'26 study finds median slowdowns of ~10–18% on the best devices, ~22% at 275 ns and ~45% at 390 ns, with a long tail [P]11. The 390 ns point probably comes from Melody's FPGA device via Octopus, so it is not independent evidence.

"Far" names a role

GB200 NVL72's capacity tier is 17 TB of LPDDR5X that NVIDIA lists as "CPU memory", coherent with the GPUs over NVLink-C2C [V]50. It sits beside the Grace CPUs, centimetres from the GPU package, and serves the role a far tier serves: large, cheaper, low bandwidth per GB. (Its exact physical placement on the module is not stated in the specification page we stored; the deck's wording follows reviewer knowledge.) Vera's 1.5 TB SOCAMM LPDDR5X is socketed, not on-package [V]31.

03 · Pressure 1: bandwidth → A

Pressure 1: bandwidth crosses a boundary

Bandwidth must cross a boundary; closer means more wires and fewer pJ/bit, until the die edge.

Two relations

Bandwidth = (wires crossing a boundary) × (bit rate per wire) Memory power = Bandwidth × (energy per bit) P [W] = BW [TB/s] × 8 × E [pJ/bit] (1 TB/s × 1 pJ/bit = 8 W)

Each factor is set by the boundary [Syn]. Wire count scales with the boundary's size divided by the pitch: through an edge it grows with perimeter, through a face with area ÷ pitch². Package balls sit about 1 mm apart [background]; HBM microbumps were 48 × 55 µm in an HBM1-era design [P]24; hybrid bonds reach 3 µm pitch and 110,000 vias/mm² [P]24. (d-Matrix's 36 µm is a 3D face-to-face bond, so it does not belong in the interposer row [V]27.) Per-wire rate is limited by loss, reflections and crosstalk, which grow with length and with connectors; serial links buy rate with equalization energy, 11.4 pJ/bit per lane at 37 dB channel loss for an Intel 10 nm PCIe Gen5 SerDes [P, measured]26. Energy per bit is roughly switched capacitance × V², and capacitance grows with wire length.

Compute grows with transistor count, which is area; DRAM bandwidth to a socket grows with perimeter. Berger et al. put it as "socket performance scales with chip area (n²) while DDR scales with perimeter" [P]11. Gholami et al. measure the result: peak hardware FLOPS grew 3.0× every two years over twenty years (60,000× in total), DRAM bandwidth 1.6× (100×) and interconnect bandwidth 1.4× (30×) [P]21. The stated totals check: 3.010 = 59,049, 1.610 = 110, 1.410 = 28.9 [D]. Compute outgrows DRAM bandwidth by about 3.0/1.6 ≈ 1.9× every two years [D].

Two caveats. The paper does not say which DRAM products form its 1.6× line (the labelled points mix GDDR and HBM), and its 410× per two years for model parameters is fitted mostly on 2018–22 [P]21. We did not use Micron's Hot Chips 2026 memory-wall chart: it fits 3× against <2× per two years with no stated data sources or normalization, from an interested seller [V]37.

Why the interposer helps, and where it stops

Off-package, a DDR5 channel carries 64 data bits through ~1 mm balls, centimetres of board and a connector, and costs a CPU about 160–200 signal pins [P]15,9. On a silicon interposer one HBM4 stack gets 2,048 data wires. Per attachment that is 2,048/64 = 32× the wires. MI455X delivers 23.3 TB/s from 12 HBM4 stacks [V]29, so ≈1.94 TB/s per stack, ≈38× a DDR5-6400 channel's 51.2 GB/s, at ≈1.94 × 8 / 2,048 ≈ 7.6 Gb/s per pin against DDR5-6400's 6.4 [D from V]. Nearly all the gain is width.

The width comes with a short reach. HBM uses "simple unterminated signaling" that "can travel roughly 5 to 7 mm on an interposer" at 2 Gb/s; wires that reach farther must be thicker and more widely spaced, so "the achievable bandwidth density along the edge of the processor will decrease proportionally" [P]22 (§3.5, p.8). The host's HBM PHY therefore sits at a die edge facing the stack, and the stack count is set by edge length. The usual phrasing, "the interposer solves wires per stack and beachfront limits stacks per die", describes one limit twice: wires per mm of die edge at a given reach [Syn]38.

Power is a co-limit and a budget

Each HBM generation has cut energy per bit more slowly than it has raised bandwidth, so package memory power climbs from about 50 W (HBM1) to about 350 W (HBM4 projection) along Fig. 3c [D]. Reaching 100 TB/s would take 1.92 kW of memory power at 2.4 pJ/bit, "for HBM power only (excluding fabric power)" [V]27, about 1.15–1.25 kW if the historical trend continued [D], and about 3.4–4 kW at the 40 mW per effective GB/s (≈4.25–5 pJ/bit) that d-Matrix's s28 table uses for HBM4, whose scope is not stated [D from V]. For scale, Samsung's example GPU draws 1,200 W of compute power plus about 220 W of DRAM at ~14 TB/s [V]28. O'Connor et al. made the same point in 2017: a 60 W DRAM budget makes "systems with more than 2 TB/s of bandwidth" impossible with HBM2 [P, modelled]22.

So power has bound alongside the edge limit all along. It differs from the edge limit in kind: it is a budget, and every watt spent on DRAM is a watt the package cannot spend on compute. Samsung's zHBM projection, four DRAM stacks hybrid-bonded onto the xPU, reads as ≈31.8 TB/s at ≈124 W against ≈13.9 TB/s at ≈218 W for the 2.5D baseline (pixel reads of an unlabelled chart), and Samsung moves the ~100 W it saves into the GPU (1,200 → 1,300 W) [V, projection]28.

After the interposer, energy sits in the DRAM core

In HBM2 the DRAM core (activation plus on-die movement) is 3.45 of 3.92 pJ/bit, 88% [D from P]. At 100 TB/s those two terms alone would take 3.45 × 8 × 100 ≈ 2.8 kW [D]. Shortening the wire further therefore has limited leverage on total power. FGDRAM's 1.95 pJ/bit (against 3.83 for its quad-bandwidth HBM baseline) came from cutting activation and datapath energy, with I/O almost unchanged (0.77 vs 0.80 pJ/bit at 50% activity) [P, modelled]22.

The same comparison, done like for like, bounds what 3D stacking buys at the interface. HBM2's I/O channel costs 0.80 pJ/bit at 50% toggling and about 0.3 at real toggle rates [P]22; an HBM1-era interface including its PHY about 1.5 pJ/bit [P, cited]24. Raptor's 0.37 pJ/bit of measured I/O is therefore about 2–4× lower I/O [D from V], and UniIC's hybrid-bonded interfaces report 0.88 and 0.66 pJ/bit I/O [P]24,25. d-Matrix's "~10×" compares Raptor's I/O figure with its HBM4 "system" figure (2.5 pJ/bit, or 5.5 with on-chip delivery) [V], which mixes scopes. 04 · Pressure 2: capacity cost → B

Pressure 2: capacity cost

Capacity cost now bites: $/bit declines slowed ~2.5× and memory is 37% of Meta's rack TCO.

Bits stopped getting cheap fast

AI Impacts fitted two DRAM price series. McCallum's retail data fall about 36% per year on average and only about 15% per year from 2010 onwards; Objective Analysis's series fall 33% and 12% per year [S]39. (The 36% and 33% are whole-series averages; only the post-2010 rates are fitted to a stated period. All four are in 2020 dollars.) Jim Handy dates the break to "2011 or so" [S]41. The ratio of rates is 36/15 = 2.4 and 33/12 = 2.75, so the decline slowed about 2.5× [D]. Our own least-squares fits to the nominal annual series plotted in Fig. 9 give −37%/yr for 1990–2010 and −12%/yr for 2010–24 [D]; they sit within the published CPI-adjusted ranges, and the post-2010 slope is sensitive to the break year (−10%/yr if the fit starts in 2011, −13%/yr if it ends in 2017). The cause is density: SemiAnalysis describes DRAM density as roughly doubling per decade now, against about 100× per decade historically [S]42.

Meanwhile demand per socket kept rising with core counts. Berger et al. find that capacity per unit of CPU performance "dropped by nearly 60–70%" over ten years; current AMD and Intel parts offer 27% and 39% of their former capacity per unit performance [P]11. Capacity is also bought in coarse steps. One DIMM per channel is the rated configuration for DDR5; CPUs with 128–288 cores need 3DS DIMMs, "which adds a multiplicative factor to per-GB memory cost" [P, qualitative]45. Fastswap's authors note that the smallest balanced upgrade of a 12 × 16 GB server is +25% and the next +50% [P]6.

Memory is a large and growing share of cost

The share is not monotonic: TCO share falls from 25.5% (Gen1) to 21.2% (Gen3) before reaching 37.1% at Gen5 [P]. Other operators report carbon rather than cost. DIMMs are 69% of per-server lifecycle emissions at Meta in Vistara's accounting [P]4; GreenSKU puts DRAM at 35% of compute-server emissions and Memstrata at 41% of embodied carbon [P]46,45. Their accounting boundaries differ and were not reconciled. The frequently quoted "DRAM is 50% of server cost" for Azure is circular: Berger (2023) cites Pond, Pond cites a 2020 Next Platform article, and Berger's cost model then assumes "approximately half" [P for the chain]10,7. We do not use it.

Much of the memory bought is idle

Four quantities are often merged; they must stay separate.

  • Stranded memory is unallocated because cores ran out first. In Azure's 100 clusters over 75 days, the daily average is 6% when 75% of cores are scheduled and over 10% at ~85%; the 95th percentile reaches 25% under high utilization [P, measured]7.
  • Cold memory is allocated but not touched within a window. Google finds 32% of memory usage cold at T = 120 s, ranging from 1% to 61% across clusters [P, measured]43.
  • Idle memory in TPP's sense: 55–80% of an application's allocated memory is idle within any two-minute interval in four Meta applications [P, measured]8.
  • Captured memory is what production systems actually move to a cheaper tier: Google's zswap covers about 20% of data with a 0.2%/min promotion-rate SLO and saves 4–5% of memory TCO [P, production]43; Meta's TMTS puts 25% of DRAM on a slower tier at under 5% slowdown [P, production]44; TPP runs 33% of memory on a CXL-like tier (2:1) at 99.5% of baseline throughput, on dual-socket machines configured to mimic CXL [P, emulated]8.

Captured (20–33%) is much smaller than idle (55–80%) for three reasons. Pages come back: "for Web, almost 80% of the pages are re-accessed within a ten-minute interval" [P]8. Migration costs CPU: Memstrata reports TPP using nearly an entire host core for one VM [P, read online]45, while TPP itself sees no meaningful overhead [P]8, an unresolved conflict. And tail-latency SLOs bound how much a promotion stall can cost [Syn].

The 2025–26 shortage is context, not cause

The cheapest branded DRAM on the spot market rose about 7× from ~$2.3/GB (July–August 2025) to ~$16/GB by late July 2026 in Handy's chart [V, chart read]36. That is a legacy-part spot series, useful as illustration only. The argument of this section rests on the decade-long measured slowdown; the spike amplifies it (§8).

Three observations

1. Deployed CXL is not a new density point. Vistara's tier (0.225) sits at capacity-maxed DDR (0.2). It moves down the cost axis at DDR-like density [Syn]. Its bandwidth is about 10× lower in absolute terms (614/57.6 = 10.7× by spec, 497/48 = 10.4× measured) but only 2.7–3.6× lower per GB (0.8 versus 0.225–0.30) [D from P]. Per bit it also costs more energy: a direct CXL access is the DDR device's 16–21 pJ/bit plus 6–23 pJ/bit per link traversal, so ~22–44 pJ/bit direct and ~28–67 switched, excluding controller and switch cores, about 1.3–2× local DDR system [D from P]26,23. Its energy case exists only at system level: fewer idle DIMMs and reused media.

The recycled-DDR4 cost. Vistara's Table V gives 0.13× $/GB for its tier [P]4. Reconstructed, that is roughly a $300 CXL ASIC plus a $210 ×16 card per 256 GB, ≈$2/GB, against DDR5 at the paper's assumed $15/GB [D]. Vistara actually uses two ASICs and two ×8 cards per 256 GB, which doubles the hardware to ≈$4/GB [D]. Counting the resale value of the pulled DDR4 ($5–9/GB) as an opportunity cost gives $7–11/GB all-in, 0.3–0.6× new DDR5 at $14–25/GB contract [D from S]. The stated input ranges span 0.28–0.79; 0.3–0.6 is the central band.

2. The middle improved; on server CPUs it caught up with HBM. A 16-channel MRDIMM-12800 socket gives 1.6 TB/s [V]32, matching Xeon Max's HBM peak (1.64 TB/s) and about twice its measured STREAM bandwidth, at many times the capacity [D from V/P]. The two HBM server CPUs were not continued. Diamond Rapids has 16 channels of DDR5-8000 or MRDIMM-12800, an LLC of up to 1.28 GB and CXL 3.0, and no HBM [V]32. Fujitsu's MONAKA has 12 channels of DDR5-8000+, an LLC on a stacked SRAM die and PCIe 6.0/CXL 3.0 [V]33. Vendors give no reason, and four explanations compete [Syn]: bandwidth-hungry HPC moved to attached GPUs (Fujitsu's roadmap pairs the later MONAKA-X with NVLink Fusion); HBM is rationed and priced for GPU buyers (§8); Intel cut its roadmap; or HBM-on-CPU survives as APUs such as MI300A [unv.]. The pattern is consistent with a hierarchy widening around the middle. It does not prove a market preference.

3. DDR is squeezed. On one DDR bus, capacity and bandwidth trade off: bigger DIMMs dilute GB/s per GB (0.8 → 0.2 from 64 to 256 GB DIMMs), and full speed needs one DIMM per channel. Two wiring regimes are stable: wide parallel wiring at millimetre reach (HBM's 1,024–2,048 bits; hybrid bonds at 110,000 vias/mm²) and serial links at ~4× bandwidth per pin per direction with metre reach and +50–70 ns per hop. Serial memory attach has a long graveyard: FB-DIMM, RDRAM and HMC [background; partly unv.], and OMI, which shipped in POWER10 and folded into CXL in 2022 [Pr]. CXL Type-3 is the first to reach volume [Syn].

The 3D row. Raptor's ~3,100 GB/s per GB is a vendor peak from slide 13 (chiplet 4 GB at 12.5 TB/s; card 32 GB at 100 TB/s), read from the slide image [D from V]27. On s28 d-Matrix states its effective-bandwidth basis as 83% for Raptor and 85% for Rubin, and its s21 discusses a naive design that would waste 33 TB/s on overfetch before the "stream blocking" fix. (Our data file's note that "press says ~33 TB/s practical" misreads that slide; the figure is waste in the naive design.) Per area, Raptor offers about 20× HBM4's bandwidth density (32.6 vs 1.39–1.67 GB/s per mm²) at 0.43–0.52× its capacity density [V].

Low intensity alone does not decide placement. Embedding gathers have the lowest intensity of the recommendation operators, 0.25 FLOP/B (RNN 5.5, FC 18, CNN 141) [P]59, yet "the majority of the larger capacity embedding tables have a relatively low bandwidth requirement, and the tables that contribute to the most bandwidth have small sizes" [P]60. Read and write density differ. HeMem's table gives DDR4 at 107/80 GB/s (read/write) against Optane at 32/11.2 GB/s, and HeMem migrates write-heavy pages to DRAM first [P]62.

A worked instance. ZeRO-Infinity models training efficiency per data class as ait·bw / (ait·bw + peak), with intensity seq×bsz for parameters and gradients, seq×bsz/4 for optimizer state and 24×hd×ci for activation checkpoints. On a V100 the three classes need ">70 GB/s, 1.5 TB/s, and 1–4 GB/s" of bandwidth; the optimizer state needs more than HBM provides, so its step runs at the CPU and NVMe tier [P]63. That is the placement rule applied per object, and it is why training already runs compute at the capacity tier (§8).

Economic rules. The five-minute rule asks when it pays to keep a page in the faster tier; DRAM↔flash break-even fell from about 15 minutes (2007) to about 41 s for 4 KB NVMe pages (2017) [P]52, and a 2025 preprint gives ~34 s at 512 B for CPU+DDR and ~5 s for GPU+GDDR with high-IOPS SSDs [P, preprint, modelled]53. Hao et al. argue the rule does not fit remote memory, which is "not cheap enough to be a storage device, nor is it fast enough to be a performance device", and instead add remote memory "until it saturates the remote memory bandwidth" [P, emulated]56. The middle tier needs a bandwidth term (Appendix F).

Counter-evidence and limits

Colloid's loaded-latency result (§2) means the hottest pages are not always best placed in the nominally fastest tier [P]18. Pond finds that "the variability within each workload class is typically much higher than across workload classes" [P]7, which supports per-object placement and argues against per-class rules. In deployed tiered systems the DDR middle still holds 50–75% of capacity: Vistara 75% and TMTS 75% in production, TPP 67% in its emulated 2:1 configuration, Memstrata 50% as a research design [D from P]4,44,8,45; Berger finds that "more than 25% of datacenter memory needs to remain local" [P, modelled]10.

07 · Deployed vs ambitious

Deployed versus ambitious

The deployed forms need no new programming model; the ambitious forms remain niche.

Each direction splits in two. A1 brings memory closer for bandwidth with unchanged software; A2 moves compute into the memory. B1 makes bytes cheaper or extends them inside one host; B2 shares them across hosts. The split explains more than the A/B axis does, because the deployed and ambitious cells fail for mirror-image reasons [Syn].

A1 in detail

The evidence is the bandwidth density and energy of §3 and §5. Two vendor positions conflict on what comes next. d-Matrix's Raptor holds about half HBM4's capacity per mm² [V]27. In the GTC'26 conversation, a speaker calls stacked DRAM "much more capacity and cheaper cost per bit" [V]75; the comparison in that passage is with putting everything in SRAM, and the AI-generated transcript does not label whether the speaker is Dally or Dean. The same conversation describes stacking DRAM on the chip as "not quite ready", with "an order of magnitude more bandwidth with an order of magnitude less energy per bit", a roadmap remark [V]. Thermal resistance improves with packaging: SK hynix shows MR-MUF at 0.45–0.55× of the HBM2 baseline and hybrid bonding at 0.40× under a "Development" banner [V]35. Base-die logic is the part of A1 moving fastest (custom HBM4 base dies; §3).

A2 in detail

AttAcc's baselines [P, sim.]66. Against 2×DGX, which has the same 80 HBM stacks as DGX+AttAcc, throughput is up to 1.72× (GPT-3 175B). Against DGX_Large (1,280 GB, 40 stacks, the same stack count as AttAcc alone) it is 2.81× (LLaMA-65B), 2.39× (GPT-3) and 2.01× (MT-NLG 530B). Against the 640 GB DGX_Base it is 3.49–5.93×, part of which is extra KV capacity (KV space grows 2.3× to 5.4×). The gain "diminishes" with larger GQA groups because the GPU reuses KV in cache. The per-bank units take 13.12 mm², 10.84% of a 121 mm² HBM3 die, or about 25% if sized like HBM-PIM units. A "3.19×/3.22×" figure circulating for AttAcc is not in the paper [unv.].

Other PIM results. Newton is 54× faster than a realistic GPU but only 10× faster than an ideal non-PIM design with infinite compute and perfect external bandwidth [P, sim.]70. UPMEM on real DIMMs is 23.2× (2,556 DPUs) or 10.1× (640 DPUs) faster than a 4-core Xeon E3-1240, with 1.64× better energy on average [P, measured]71; its logic runs at 350 MHz on "3x slower transistors" with "3 metals only for routing" [V]72. SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM claims "up to a 10× speed improvement" from FPGA emulation, with no energy figure in the paper [P]67. Samsung's LPDDR5X-PIM shows 27.0 → 81.3 tokens/s (>3×) on Llama-3.1-8B on its own edge SoC, with no energy data [V]69. Boroumand et al. find 62.7% of mobile system energy is data movement, and their PIM results (−55.4% energy and +54.2% performance for PIM accelerators; −49.1% and +44.6% for PIM cores) are gem5-simulated [P, sim.]73.

Costs. Samsung's HBM2-PIM stack holds 6 GB against 8 GB for plain HBM2 (four of its eight dies carry compute), from the comparison table in the AiM paper [P]67; Kwon ISSCC'21, which would give the PIM unit's area and measured pJ/bit, is [pend]. SK hynix's VP of system architecture said PIM "may serve niche workloads, it is not a broad-spectrum fix", because logic area "inherently reduces the space available to store actual data" [Pr]74; SK hynix is an interested party betting on packaging and has itself invested in PIM. Refresh doubles per ~10 °C above 85 °C [P]104, and PIM units have no virtual memory or coherence. UPMEM was reportedly acquired by Qualcomm in 2025 [S, date conflicts].

B1 in detail

Compression is the oldest B1. IBM's MXT reached "typical ratios" of 2:1–6:1; halving installed memory in a Compaq DL360 cut the system price from $13,081 to $7,103, over 40%, at near-equal SPECint2000 [P, priced model]77. Google's zswap moves about 20% of data to a tier at least 67% cheaper, saving 4–5% of memory TCO [P]43; TMTS replaces 25% of DRAM at under 5% slowdown [P]44. Vistara runs recycled DDR4 behind CXL on "millions of servers" [P]4. Azure's M-series is the first announced deployment of CXL expansion [Pr], and Octopus states expansion is deployed in Azure production [P]2.

The costs are real. ByteDance measures Spark 1.4–9.8× slower when its memory is interleaved across DRAM and CXL; its "25.98% TCO" saving comes from a cost model with example parameters [P; TCO modelled]12. For equal purchased capacity, local DRAM beats far memory by 2.5% (+25% capacity) and 9.7% (+50%) in Fastswap's rack simulation [P, sim.]6. GreenSKU's CXL tier with reused DDR4 nets only +1% carbon (+11% embodied, −6% operational) [P, modelled]46. Optane is the lesson: Intel began winding it down in 2022 with a $559M inventory impairment [P]78, after reportedly selling at about half of DRAM $/GB while costing more to make [S]. A B1 tier must cost less to make as well as to sell.

The pattern

The deployed cells (A1, B1) need no programming-model change. Computational storage is the precedent: it had products, but "several challenges prevented the broad usage and adoption of computational SSDs" [P]81.

09 · Why both, why now

Why both, why now

The 1990s had the same split; commodity DRAM let the middle outrun both, until ~2011.

The 1990s split

Both directions were pursued in the 1990s, often by the same people [P]: Patterson and Anderson wrote both IRAM (1997) and NOW (1995)95,96; Oskin worked on Active Pages and later Grappa; J. Chang on memory blades and the 2014 NDP report100; Ranganathan on memory blades and Nanostores, which is near-data compute plus scale-out, a "hierarchy of computes"102.

A's target was the off-chip bus. Fromm et al. measured 98.5 nJ for an off-chip L1-line access against 4.55 nJ on chip, about 22× [P]94. IRAM cited an AlphaServer's 253 ns memory latency against ≈60 ns raw DRAM, and 100–200 GB/s of internal bandwidth against 1.2 GB/s at the pins; merged DRAM-logic processes cost "30% to 70%" in area and "30% to 100%" in speed [P]95. IRAM aimed at games, PDAs and embedded devices.

B's target was disk. GMS fetched a remote page in 1,440 µs against 3,600–14,300 µs from disk [P]97; NOW put network RAM at "10% to 30% slower" than local and "5 to 10 times faster than thrashing to disk" [P]96, aimed at science clusters. IVY (1989) had already shown the workload boundary: super-linear speedup when data outgrew one node, and "no speedup at all" for a read-once dot product [P]98. Hardware DSM machines ran remote/local latency ratios of 2.9× (HP Exemplar), ≈3.6× (SGI Origin, 710 vs 200 ns), 4.4× (HaL) and 14.5× (DG) [P]99; CXL.mem's ratio is about 2× (140 → 280 ns in Levis) [P]1, below SGI Origin's.

The 2014 NDP workshop report states the common factor for the PIM machines of the 1990s (DIVA, Terasys, EXECUBE): they "did not fade away because of lack of performance gains or energy efficiency, but simply because Moore's law allowed the basic von Neumann machines to outrun them with economic affordability" [P]100. The least disruptive forms survived: hardware CC-NUMA, eDRAM caches, HBM over HMC.

What differs now

Since about 2011 the DRAM $/bit decline has run at roughly a third of its earlier rate (§4), so neither end can be outrun by buying more commodity DRAM [Syn]. Scaling limits were forecast before: Wulf and McKee predicted the memory wall, a processor–DRAM speed gap, in 1995 [P]101, and Ranganathan in 2011 foresaw "a slowing of DRAM growth due to scaling challenges" and bet on PCM and memristors, which did not arrive [P]102. The difference now is a decade of measured slowdown, as opposed to a forecast or the 2026 price spike. Four enablers are also new [Syn]: packaging that decouples DRAM and logic processes (SeDRAM bonds a 25 nm DRAM array to a 55 nm logic wafer [P]24; HBM4 base dies move to foundry logic [V]); an anchor workload, AI, on vendor-controlled stacks; a CXL standard that absorbed Gen-Z, OpenCAPI and OMI; and hyperscalers that own their operating systems. Aguilera et al.'s test, that success "depended on two requirements: addressing a burning issue and being technically feasible", fits B [P]80; the paper does not discuss near-memory compute.

Rival explanations, weighed

(a) Different markets. True and first-order, then and now: A's evidence comes mostly from accelerators and mobile, B's from CPU cloud. The strongest both-at-once cases are single workloads (LLM serving, recommendation models, Chrome's ZRAM, whose compression is itself a PIM target [P]73, and GB200). Markets differ because their binding constraint differs, so the market explanation and the resource explanation are compatible [Syn].

(b) Vendor incentives. Real and complementary. Memory makers want out of commodity pricing (custom HBM base dies; Samsung's PIM and CXL-PNM roadmap; SK hynix's packaging bet); controller vendors and start-ups gain a product category. The residue of hype (HMC, The Machine, Gen-Z, RSA) shows that incentives alone do not make a direction work [Syn].

(c) Different communities. Context more than cause. PIM papers lean on simulation with weak baselines; disaggregation papers lean on production traces but model their TCO. Roscoe et al. observe that "very little of this work has found its way to product" [P]103. No paper we found treats near and far as one quantitative axis; IRAM, Nanostores and the 2014 NDP report sketch it qualitatively.

What the slide simplified. The slide's chart labels are our nominal fits (−37%/yr, −12%/yr) while its table gives the published CPI-adjusted rates; both are stated here. Its "same people" list compresses careers that spanned both directions at different times. Its "middle outran both" is our synthesis; the NDP report says it only of the PIM machines.

10 · Implications

Implications

Specify memory by frontier position, not attach point; three observations would refute this account.

A spec sheet that fits the frontier. For any memory product, state GB/s per GB (peak per direction, and sustained), $/GB, pJ/bit with its scope (I/O, full access or system), and idle and loaded latency. Attach point ("near", "far", "on-package", "pooled") predicts none of these well (§2, §5). On server CPUs, any product between the DIMM and HBM must beat MRDIMM-12800 plus a large SRAM LLC plus CXL, the combination both post-HBM server CPUs chose (§5).

Open questions

  1. Where is pooling's break-even (Levis's 12.6 TB; Berger's share-of-BOM model) at 2026–27 DRAM prices?
  2. Does an HBM-parity floor bind under DDR prices in 2027 (Appendix E)?
  3. Can ρ, I and MLP be combined into a quantitative placement model that includes Hao's bandwidth-saturation rule?
  4. Can 3D DRAM–logic stacking's capacity cost be offset at system level by LPDDR or CXL tiers, as d-Matrix already does?
  5. What workload, if any, would justify HBM on a server CPU again?

Tensions to watch

PCIe lanes. CXL used for bandwidth expansion needs many lanes: Micron and Intel's Xeon 6900P study adds eight ×8 CXL devices (64 PCIe lanes, 205 GB/s) to 12 DDR5-6400 channels for +24% read bandwidth with tuned 3:1 interleaving, while naive 1:1 interleaving reduces bandwidth [P]105. Those are the lanes GPUs and NICs use in AI servers. Rack power. Vistara's CXL tier draws 30 W per 256 GB [P]4, inside racks whose power is dominated by HBM accelerators.

What the slide simplified. The slide lists predictions without time frames; none of them is a price forecast. Its confidence labels grade the evidence behind each prediction, not the probability of the outcome.

Appendix D

What would change our mind

Three observations would count against the account (§10): CXL capacity tiers growing after DRAM $/GB returns to trend; CPU HBM returning while MRDIMM keeps scaling; PIM winning in silicon at iso-stack, iso-capacity baselines. Weaker signals would also shift the weights:

  • HBM prices fall and r falls with yield learning. The wafer coupling of §8 would weaken, and B's cheapest forms would lose some of their relative advantage.
  • 3D DRAM restores steep $/bit declines. The middle could again outrun both ends, as in the 1990s, and B's capacity case would shrink.
  • Switched CXL pools show positive ROI in production (as opposed to simulation). B2 would no longer be confined to bulk, predictable data.
  • A quantitative ρ × I × MLP model that predicts placements better than hotness. It would test the per-object claim of §6 directly.
  • Raptor's full text shows a large core-energy reduction. The 3D direction's energy case would then extend past the interface, and §3's "2–4×, not core energy" would need revising.

Appendix F

Five-minute rule and Hao et al.

Break-even history. DRAM↔flash break-even intervals fell from ~15 minutes (2007) to ~40 s, 41 s for 4 KB NVMe pages (2017) [P]52. Zhang (2025) gives ~34 s for CPU+DDR at 512 B and ~5 s on GPU hosts with high-IOPS SSDs [P, preprint, modelled]53; the page sizes differ, so the two are not a single series.

Hao et al.56 argue that remote memory is "not cheap enough to be a storage device, nor is it fast enough to be a performance device", and that the five-minute rule "assumes unlimited bandwidth for the performance device" (§4.9, p.31:12). Their provisioning rule: size local DRAM by the classic rule, then "gradually replace local memory with remote memory until it saturates the remote memory bandwidth" (p.31:12). The introduction states the rule in the reverse direction (prefer remote memory, then replace it with local for higher performance targets; p.31:2).

Evidence quality [P, emulated]. Remote memory is emulated by no-op injection (§5.2): 1 µs and 5 GB/s by default, swept over 0.1–5 µs, on one node, with a zipf-0.9 benchmark of 23 GB (3 GB local, 18 GB remote, 10 threads). Prices are relative only. The "up to 10×" gain in the introduction is against a two-tier buffer manager, while §4.9 says "more than 10×" against memory plus disk. Some three-tier configurations cost more than two-tier ones at 0.8× price (p.31:21). Skew carries the result: local memory one sixth the size of remote serves more than half the accesses, and throughput stays at ~70% at 50× remote latency for all designs except one (p.31:16).
